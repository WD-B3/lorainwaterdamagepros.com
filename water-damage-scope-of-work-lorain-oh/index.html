Water Damage Scope of Work: What to Expect in Lorain, OH

A scope of work is the written plan for a job. This guide explains its parts so you can tell a clear plan from a vague one.

Call (440) 276-9091See all services

By the Lorain Water Damage Pros team. Published November 9, 2026.

A scope of work is the list of tasks a company will perform and the conditions that apply to them. For water damage, it connects what was found, what will be done and how success will be measured. A clear scope protects you and the company. A vague one is where disagreements start.

The parts of a good scope

  1. A description of the loss and its cause.
  2. A moisture map showing which rooms and materials are wet.
  3. The water category and how it was determined.
  4. A removal list for materials that cannot be dried.
  5. A drying plan with equipment and expected days.
  6. Cleaning and treatment steps.
  7. Repair and rebuild items, separated from emergency work.
  8. Verification, such as final moisture readings.

Each item should be specific. "Remove wet drywall" is weaker than "remove drywall on the north wall of the living room up to 24 inches above the floor." Specific scopes can be checked later against what was done.

The moisture map

A moisture map is a drawing or table showing where readings were high. It is created with a moisture meter and often a thermal camera, which shows temperature differences caused by wet materials. The map is a baseline for drying and proof of where water traveled.

Moisture mapShows where water went, including hidden areas

Scope versus the insurer's version

Insurers often write their own estimate. It may list fewer items than the restoration scope, or it may price items differently. Compare the two line by line and ask the insurer to explain each difference.

Moisture readings and photos are the best evidence for items that are disputed. If a wall was opened because readings were high, the reading is the reason. Keep the logs, since they are the strongest support for your scope.

Local rules that shape the scope

Some repair steps need permits, and the rules vary by town. Lorain's Building Division issues plumbing, electrical and demolition permits, and Sheffield Lake requires permits for items like water heater and roof work. Amherst issues permits through an online portal.

If a permit is required, the scope should say who pulls it and when inspections happen. That detail belongs in the written plan, not in a phone call.

Questions a scope should answer

  • What caused the loss, and has the source been stopped?
  • Which rooms and materials are wet, and how wet?
  • What is being removed, and why can it not be dried?
  • How many drying units are planned and for how long?
  • What will be measured to show the job is finished?

If a scope cannot answer these in plain language, ask the company to revise it. The questions are not hostile, and a good contractor will have the answers ready.

A sample of how specific lines read

  • Extract standing water from the finished basement, about 600 square feet, and lift the carpet and pad.
  • Install four air movers and one dehumidifier in the basement for an expected three to five days, with daily readings.
  • Remove drywall along the east wall to 24 inches above the floor and discard wet insulation.
  • Clean and treat exposed framing and the slab surface.
  • Repair and rebuild items to be priced after the drying is verified.

These lines are examples of the level of detail to expect, not a quote for any property. Every loss has its own numbers. The point is that each line can be checked afterward against what was done.

What to do when the scope changes: change orders

Sometimes the scope changes because new damage is found. A change order is a written update that describes the change, the reason and any price effect. Do not accept verbal changes, since they are hard to document later.

  • Ask for the change in writing before work continues.
  • Request photos or readings that show the reason for the change.
  • Share the change with your insurer promptly.
  • Keep a copy with the original scope.

Drying before rebuilding

A scope that rebuilds before drying is verified is a red flag. Wet framing behind new drywall can turn into a new problem. Look for a line that ties the rebuild to final moisture readings. The structural drying page explains how those readings are taken.

The same advice holds for homeowners in Vermilion and Amherst, where permit rules and water sources differ but the logic of a scope does not.
