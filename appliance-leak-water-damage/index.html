Close the shutoff valve behind or beside the appliance, unplug it only if the floor around the plug is dry and flip the breaker if it is not. Pull the appliance forward only after the water is off, because hoses can still be under pressure.

Which appliances cause the most trouble

The worst losses come from supply hoses that burst under pressure, since they keep delivering water until someone closes the valve. Braided hoses fail at the crimp, and rubber hoses age and split. Check yours when you replace an appliance, and shut the valve when you leave for a long trip.

Slow leaks hide the most damage

A refrigerator line that drips a little at a time can soak the subfloor beneath the appliance. A dishwasher gasket that weeps into the cabinet base can warp the toe kick and swell the particleboard. Signs include a musty smell, a floor that feels soft near the appliance, lifting vinyl edges and a rising water bill.

Moisture meters and thermal cameras find water under flooring and behind base cabinets without tearing anything out. That is how crews decide what can be dried and what has to be removed.

What to do, and what the cleanup involves

  1. Stop the leak and move the appliance out of the way.
  2. Extract standing water and lift any soaked floor covering.
  3. Check subfloor, cabinet bases and nearby walls for hidden moisture.
  4. Set air movers and dehumidifiers, with specialty drying under cabinets if needed.
  5. Record readings until the materials reach dry reference values.

What appliance leak cleanup costs depend on

  • How long it leaked. A burst hose for ten minutes and a drip for three weeks are very different.
  • Flooring type. Vinyl, tile and laminate hide water differently from carpet.
  • Cabinetry. Particleboard bases swell and often cannot be dried.
  • Rooms below. A laundry room over a finished room adds ceiling work.
  • Appliance repair. A technician or plumber prices the fix separately.
