Why commercial losses are different

Commercial buildings have bigger floors, longer pipe runs and tenants whose work stops when the water arrives. A leak above a ceiling grid can reach a dozen offices. A sprinkler head or a failed water line in a shared riser can reach several units at once.

Stock, records and equipment add a second layer to the loss. Paper, cardboard, electronics and fabric are often the first to be damaged, and their loss drives business interruption far more than a wet carpet tile.

What a commercial response includes

  • A walk-through with the manager or owner to set priorities, such as inventory, servers and public areas.
  • Water extraction and removal of wet ceiling tile, insulation and floor coverings.
  • Drying with air movers and dehumidifiers sized for large open areas.
  • Moisture mapping and daily readings, recorded for the file.
  • Coordination with property managers, tenants and adjusters.

Records and permits

Your insurer, your landlord and sometimes the city will want to see what happened and what was done. Ask the crew for photos, moisture logs and a written scope. Lorain routes permits and contractor registration through its building office, so ask there whether repair work such as electrical, plumbing or demolition needs a permit. Owners and tenants in the central district can start with the Downtown Lorain page.

Commercial policies vary a lot. Some include business interruption, and some exclude flood or sewer backup unless added. Read yours, and keep receipts for emergency purchases such as fans, tarps and temporary storage.

What to do in the first hour: a checklist for managers

  • Shut off the water at the main or the riser valve.
  • Cut power to wet areas from the panel, only if the panel is dry and reachable.
  • Move servers, files and stock above the water line.
  • Tell tenants or staff which areas are closed.
  • Take wide photos and close-ups with the date visible.
  • Call your insurer and the property manager, then call dispatch.

A written list on the wall near the main shutoff saves minutes when staff change. Mark valves clearly, and make sure someone on every shift knows where they are. The same list works for a restaurant, a salon or a small warehouse.

What commercial cleanup costs depend on

  • Square footage affected. Large floors need more equipment.
  • Materials. Ceiling grid, carpet tile, drywall and insulation each have different options.
  • Water category. Sewage or outside water adds removal.
  • Hours and access. Working after hours to protect operations changes scheduling.
  • Contents. Stock and equipment moves add time.
