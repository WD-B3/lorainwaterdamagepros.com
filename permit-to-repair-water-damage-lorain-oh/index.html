Do You Need a Permit to Repair Water Damage in Lorain?

Drying a wet floor rarely needs a permit, but some repairs do. This guide explains how to find out before work starts.

Call (440) 276-9091See all services

By the Lorain Water Damage Pros team. Published November 9, 2026.

Whether a water damage repair needs a permit depends on what is being done, not on the fact that there was water. Drying and cleaning generally do not need one. New plumbing, electrical work, demolition and structural changes often do. The City of Lorain Building Division at City Hall is the office to ask for a Lorain address.

What the Lorain Building Division handles

The Building Division, located at City Hall on West Erie Avenue, issues plumbing, electrical and demolition permits. It also takes the application for a foundation repair or waterproofing permit and handles contractor registration. Streamlined codes took effect on March 1, 2024, so older advice may be out of date.

Contractors and registration

Lorain handles contractor registration through the Building Division. Ask any contractor whether they are registered with the city for the work in your scope. A registered contractor can usually pull permits and schedule inspections.

Nearby towns have their own offices

Each city issues its own permits, so a rule in Lorain does not carry over to a neighbor. Sheffield Lake requires permits for sewer and water line replacement, roof work, water heater replacement and electrical work, and contractors register every year. The city also requires a point-of-sale inspection.

Amherst issues building, electrical, plumbing and HVAC permits through an online portal and requires all contractors to register. Plumbing inspections are held on certain weekday evenings. For Vermilion, ask the city building department directly.

Why permits matter after water damage

A permit connects the work to an inspection. That inspection can confirm that new plumbing is installed correctly and that wiring in a once-wet area is safe. It also leaves a record, which can help when you sell or file an insurance claim.

Skipping a required permit can cause trouble later, including problems at a sale. The basement page and the water heater page describe two repairs where this question comes up often.

Fees and timing

Permit fees and review times differ by city and by project. Some small permits are issued quickly, while larger ones take days. Ask for the fee and the expected turnaround when you call, and build that time into the repair schedule.

If water damage left a house unlivable, tell the office. Some jobs need to move faster, and staff may be able to point you to the quickest route.

A simple way to approach it

  1. Write down the exact work planned, room by room.
  2. Call the Building Division and ask whether each item needs a permit.
  3. Ask what documents and fees are required.
  4. Ask your contractor who will apply and when inspections occur.
  5. Keep copies of permits and inspection results.

This sequence takes a little time and can prevent a stop-work order. A written answer from the office is better than a phone memory. If the office gives conflicting answers, ask who has authority to decide.

Inspections: what to expect

An inspector checks that work matches the permit and meets the code in force. Plan on being available or having the contractor present, since inspections can be scheduled in narrow windows. Amherst, for instance, holds plumbing inspections on set weekday evenings.

If an inspection fails, the inspector lists what must be fixed and returns after it is corrected. That is normal and not a penalty. Keep the correction notice with your records.

Selling or insuring the house later

Permits and inspection records become part of a house's history. A buyer or an insurer may ask what work was done after a water loss and whether it was inspected. A file with permits, final inspections and drying logs answers those questions in a few minutes.

If you plan to sell a house that had water damage, Ohio's residential property disclosure form asks about known material defects, so records of repairs help you answer accurately.

What a restoration company does and does not do

A restoration company dries and cleans, and may do some rebuild work. It does not decide what the city requires. Treat it as a source of questions and ask the city for the answers.
